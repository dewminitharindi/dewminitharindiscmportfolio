Campus Canteen Supply Chain Mapping

Direct Observation Flow Diagramming Waste Audit Root Cause Analysis Supplier Mapping
01

Problem Statement

What is the problem?

The SLTC Research University campus canteen has no structured supply chain management system. Ingredients are ordered based on personal judgement with no historical demand data, storage conditions do not always match food safety requirements, and large quantities of cooked food are discarded at the end of every service day with no redistribution or composting pathway.

There is no record of supplier deliveries, no inventory tracking, and no feedback mechanism between daily waste and future ordering decisions. This creates a cycle of over-ordering, over-cooking, and wastage that generates unnecessary cost and environmental impact every single day.

02

Objectives

What will this project answer?

  • Map the full supply chain from supplier to waste disposal across all 6 stages
  • Identify at least 2–3 specific operational inefficiencies in the chain
  • Quantify waste levels and estimate the financial cost of current practices
  • Propose practical, low-cost improvements for each inefficiency identified
03

Methodology

How was this analysed?

Direct Observation

One-hour structured observation at the SLTC campus canteen during peak lunch service (12:00–1:00 pm). Observed ingredient handling, storage conditions, queue flow, and end-of-day disposal practices.

Staff Interview

Informal structured interview with kitchen staff covering: how ingredients are ordered, who the suppliers are, order frequency, storage methods, and what happens to leftover food at end of day.

Flow Diagram

Mapped full 6-stage supply chain: Supplier → Delivery → Storage → Preparation → Service → Waste. Annotated bottlenecks and inefficiency points directly on the diagram.

Waste Audit

Estimated daily food waste quantity and cost per day. Identified categories of wasted food (rice, curry, short eats) and cross-referenced with ordering volumes to estimate over-ordering rate.

Recommendations

Improvements for Each Inefficiency

  • Introduce a 7-day rolling sales log — record portions served each day. Review weekly to set next week's order quantities. Eliminates intuition-based over-ordering.
  • Upgrade cold storage — install a two-door under-counter chiller for perishable items (dairy, meat, vegetables). Reduces spoilage and improves food safety compliance.
  • Switch to batch cooking — prepare food in two rounds: a main batch at 11:30 am, a top-up batch only if demand warrants. Reduces end-of-day surplus significantly.
  • Partner with campus welfare unit for end-of-day redistribution of unsold food. Eliminates 100% of daily disposal waste and supports student welfare.
  • Introduce a delivery checklist — staff sign off on quantities received from supplier. Prevents unnoticed discrepancies and builds a supplier accountability record.

    Small Pharmacy Inventory Study

    ABC Classification Reorder Point Modelling Microsoft Excel Power BI Dashboard Structured Interview
    01

    Problem Statement

    What is the problem?

    A small neighbourhood pharmacy in Colombo manages over 80 SKUs with no formal inventory classification system. Fast-moving medicines such as paracetamol and antacids regularly face stock-outs because reorder decisions are made from memory, while slow-moving specialty items accumulate past their expiry dates — generating financial losses on both ends.

    Without an ABC classification framework or calculated reorder points, the pharmacy owner cannot distinguish between high-value items needing tight control and low-value items needing minimal attention. This project applies ABC analysis and inventory modelling to bring structure to their inventory management.

    02

    Objectives

    What will this project answer?

    • Classify all 80 SKUs into A, B, and C tiers by revenue contribution
    • Calculate reorder points for A-category medicines using demand and lead time data
    • Identify C-items at risk of expiry within the next 60 days
    • Build a Power BI dashboard for ongoing inventory monitoring
    • Produce a one-page case study report with actionable recommendations
    03

    Methodology

    How was this analysed?

    Field Interview (45 mins)

    Structured interview with pharmacy owner covering: top-selling medicines, slow movers, reorder process, supplier lead times, and expiry incidents. Data manually recorded and transferred to Excel.

    ABC Classification (Excel)

    Sorted 80 SKUs by annual revenue. Applied 80/20 rule: A = top 20% SKUs by cumulative revenue contribution, B = next 30%, C = remaining 50%. Pivot table used to validate classification.

    Reorder Point Formula

    Calculated for all A-items using: ROP = Avg. Daily Sales × Lead Time (days) + Safety Stock. Safety stock set at 5 days' supply for all A-items to prevent stock-out risk.

    Power BI Dashboard

    Built a 2-page Power BI report using the Excel dataset. Page 1: ABC tier overview with KPI cards. Page 2: Expiry risk table for C-items filtered by days remaining to expiry.

    05

    Recommendations

    Inventory Management Improvements

    • Differential reorder rules by tier: daily stock check for A-items, weekly for B-items, monthly review for C-items. Match monitoring effort to revenue impact.
    • Enforce ROP triggers for A-items — create a simple alert system (highlighted row in Excel) that flags items falling below their calculated reorder point.
    • Reduce C-item order quantities — negotiate smaller, more frequent deliveries from suppliers to prevent over-stocking low-demand items that expire on shelves.
    • Promote near-expiry C-items through front-of-counter placement, bundled offers, or small discounts to recover cost before items pass their expiry date.
    • Review C-item portfolio quarterly — discontinue SKUs that have not sold in 90+ days to reduce capital tied up in dead stock.

    Kaggle Supply Chain Dataset Analysis

    Kaggle Dataset Excel Data Cleaning Power BI KPI Framework Category Analysis
    01

    Problem Statement

    What is the problem?

    Supply chain managers in retail and consumer goods often lack a consolidated view of key performance indicators across product categories. Without a visual dashboard, critical patterns — seasonal stock-out spikes, lead time variance by category, and fulfilment dips — go undetected until they become revenue-impacting issues.

    This project uses a real Kaggle supply chain dataset to practise end-to-end data analytics: cleaning raw data, calculating KPIs, and presenting findings in a professional Power BI dashboard that a supply chain manager could act on.

    02

    Objectives

    What will this project answer?

    • Clean and prepare a real-world supply chain dataset for analysis (10,000+ rows)
    • Calculate core KPIs: order fulfilment rate, average lead time, stock levels by category
    • Identify underperforming product categories and the root causes of performance dips
    • Determine which SKUs are causing the most stock-out-driven revenue loss
    • Build a multi-page Power BI dashboard with actionable insights for management
    03

    Methodology

    How was this analysed?

    Dataset Source

    Downloaded "Supply Chain Analysis" dataset from Kaggle.com. Dataset: 10,000+ rows, 24 columns covering product categories, order dates, quantities, lead times, stock levels, and fulfilment status.

    Excel Data Cleaning

    Cleaned in Excel: removed 312 duplicate rows, standardised date formats across 3 columns, imputed missing values in 3% of records using category median. Created calculated columns for fulfilment status and lead time variance.

    KPI Calculation

    Calculated: order fulfilment rate (fulfilled orders ÷ total orders), average lead time per category, stock coverage days, and stock-out frequency per SKU. Used pivot tables for category-level aggregation.

    Power BI Dashboard

    Built a 3-page Power BI report: Page 1 — KPI summary cards; Page 2 — category breakdown with bar and line charts; Page 3 — quarterly trend analysis and top stock-out SKUs table.

    Recommendations

    Data-Driven Supply Chain Improvements

    • Prioritise safety stock buffers for the 3 high-impact SKUs — Retinol Night Cream, Argan Oil Shampoo, and Moisturiser Serum A — responsible for 40% of stock-out revenue loss.
    • Renegotiate Haircare supplier lead times — reduce from 21.2 days average to target ≤15 days. Long lead times are the primary driver of partial fulfilment in this category.
    • Set Skincare Q4 demand alerts based on prior year seasonality. Dashboard trend data shows consistent Q4 demand spike that current stock levels do not accommodate.
    • Replicate Cosmetics' fulfilment model (96% rate, 12-day lead time) as internal best practice — review supplier relationships and order sizing in this category as the benchmark.
    • Automate weekly dashboard refresh by connecting Power BI directly to the order management system, eliminating manual Excel data entry and enabling real-time monitoring.

    Research · Export Chain · Risk Analysis

    Sri Lanka Tea Export Supply Chain

    Secondary Research Chain Flow Mapping Risk Matrix Sri Lanka Tea Board Data Ceylon Chamber Reports
    01

    Problem Statement

    What is the problem?

    Sri Lanka's tea export supply chain is one of the most strategically important in the country, contributing significantly to national foreign exchange earnings. Yet it is a multi-tiered chain with complex dependencies — from smallholder farmers at high elevation to international buyers in Europe, Russia, and the Middle East — each stage introducing its own risks and inefficiencies.

    The chain is acutely vulnerable to weather variability, auction price fluctuations, and port logistics delays. The near-total dependence on the Colombo auction as a single selling channel creates concentration risk. This project maps the full chain and conducts a structured risk analysis to identify where the greatest vulnerabilities lie and what mitigations are available.

    02

    Objectives

    What will this project answer?

    • Map the complete Sri Lanka tea export chain from farmer to international buyer across all stages
    • Understand the role and risk of the Colombo Tea Auction as the dominant selling channel
    • Identify and rate the top supply chain risks using a structured risk matrix (likelihood × impact)
    • Propose practical mitigation strategies for each identified risk factor
    • Produce a 2–3 page research report with annotated flow diagram and risk matrix
    03

    Methodology

    How was this analysed?

    Secondary Research Sources

    Sri Lanka Tea Board annual reports, Ceylon Chamber of Commerce export data, Sri Lanka Export Development Board industry profiles, and published academic papers on tea supply chain risk.

    Chain Flow Mapping

    Constructed a 6-stage annotated flow diagram covering each actor in the chain: smallholder farmer, leaf collector/bought-leaf factory, TSHD factory, Colombo auction, licensed exporter, and international buyer.

    Risk Matrix Construction

    Identified 5 primary risk factors from literature and industry data. Rated each on a 1–5 scale for likelihood and impact. Calculated risk score (L × I) and classified as Low / Medium / High.

    Mitigation Framework

    For each High and Medium risk factor, proposed at least one practical mitigation strategy with reference to industry best practice — including forward contracts, crop insurance, and port logistics reform.

    05

    Recommendations

    Risk Mitigation Strategies

    • Diversify beyond the Colombo Auction — develop direct-trade agreements with large international buyers (supermarket chains, blending companies in EU and Middle East) to reduce the 70% auction dependency. Even shifting 20% to direct trade reduces single-channel concentration risk significantly.
    • Establish weather-linked crop insurance for smallholder farmers — subsidised through the Tea Board. Reduces financial devastation from drought years and incentivises continued investment in cultivation and plucking quality.
    • Build exporter buffer inventory — licensed exporters should maintain 2–3 weeks of stock at Colombo warehouses to absorb port congestion delays without disrupting buyer delivery schedules.
    • Negotiate long-term forward contracts with key international buyers — fix a portion of export volume at pre-agreed prices per quarter. Reduces year-on-year price volatility exposure for factories and exporters.
    • Advocate for Colombo Port priority lane for agricultural exports — coordinate with the Sri Lanka Ports Authority to create a dedicated fast-processing channel for perishable export commodities including tea, reducing port dwell time from 3–7 days toward a 24–48 hour target.
